A Solve-A-Thon 2026 project for NC DHHS

Finding the grant was never the hard part. Knowing who has to say yes is.

Every week, hundreds of federal funding opportunities post on Grants.gov. A grant writer at the NC Department of Health and Human Services can't read them all, and most of what they could read isn't theirs to win. We built a tool that sorts every opportunity by whose signature it needs: the writer's, a supervisor's, or the secretary's or legislature's. Each result shows the reason in plain language.

Understand

Built for one person: the DHHS grant writer who can't commit matching funds alone.

We started by talking to someone who writes grants, then checked what we heard against how state grants offices and federal reviewers actually decide. Our user is a mid-level grants coordinator inside NC DHHS, not leadership. They get maybe half an hour a week to triage around a hundred postings. An application takes weeks, and any money the state has to put in needs a supervisor's approval.

What a good match means to them. We wrote these down before opening the data.

  • We can actually be the applicant. Not a research grant that expects a university professor to lead it.
  • We have what it takes to run it, or know who we'd partner with. DHHS works on housing, for example, but doesn't own housing. That means a partner, and a partner means time.
  • There is enough runway. The deadline that matters is internal sign-off, not the writing.
  • The award justifies the staff weeks. An application costs about the same effort at $200,000 as at $5 million.
  • The match is survivable at the writer's level of authority. Whoever has to approve the state's share decides which pile it belongs in.

Solve

Sort by who has to sign off, not by a score.

A ranked list tells you what looks good. It doesn't tell you what you can act on. We compare what each opportunity requires (matching money, construction, a formal designation, services the agency doesn't run) with what the agency actually controls. Short written rules then put it in a pile. Anyone can read the rules, and anyone can change them.

Reading the fine print, and who it reaches

Match percentages almost never appear in the data: 6 of {{TOTAL}} rows. They're buried in the full announcement. A free AI model running on a laptop reads each announcement and has to quote its evidence.

"Projects must include financial support from sources other than Title X." From the Title X Family Planning announcement. The Grants.gov data marks it "no cost sharing."

Open any opportunity in the tool to see where the people it serves live. It shows the top counties, the split between rural, suburban and urban counties, and counts by congressional district.

Evaluate

How sure you should be, and where we'd push back on our own work.

Our team labeled 60 opportunities by hand without looking at the AI's answers, then compared. The short version: trust the facts, check the judgment calls.

Where the AI was wrong, and how we caught it

  • It said yes too easily. Our first version called wildfire and Sierra Leone programs "aligned with DHHS." We caught it by reading its first 118 answers by hand.
  • Our fix went too far. The stricter version rejected Title X, a textbook DHHS grant. Known test cases caught it.
  • It once made up evidence. It wrote its own rule where a direct quote belonged. The tool shows every quote, so it was visible.

What we didn't do, and the tradeoff

  • We didn't hand-check every Tier 1–3 result. We ran out of time. The 60 blind labels are our evidence instead, so a writer should confirm a Tier 1 result before committing staff.
  • The AI never picks the pile. Written rules do, and every card shows why. That makes mistakes easy to see and fix.
  • Some lines are assumptions. Nothing published sets the $1 million line above which a match needs an appropriation. Leadership can change it in one place.
Mobilize

Three decisions leadership could make this month.

Not a top-ten list. Each one names who has to act and by when.

  • Division of Public Health, to route it
    Start on Integrated Viral Hepatitis Surveillance and Prevention. Tier 1's top result: about $7.5 million per award (estimated), no state match, fits current staff. It's a forecast Grants.gov expects to close February 16, 2027, so Public Health can prepare before it posts.
  • The Secretary, to decide on the match
    Decide whether to fund the 30% match for the Preschool Development Grant Birth Through Five. Tier 3: up to $15 million, needs a formal state designation. Due November 20, 2026, so the decision can't wait.
  • Division directors, to line up partners
    Put standing agreements in place for research evaluation and workforce training. 17 of the 24 opportunities waiting on a supervisor are waiting on a partner DHHS doesn't have in place. Research and evaluation (9) and workforce training (6) come up most. Arranging those partners once, instead of grant by grant, shortens the path for all of them.

With more time

  • Re-read announcements every week so match percentages and deadlines update themselves.
  • Sit down with division staff to verify what DHHS runs, contracts or partners for.
  • Add past award history to estimate how competitive North Carolina would be.
