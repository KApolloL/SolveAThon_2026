## 2. The three most damaging weaknesses, with the tradeoff to name

1. **The evidence is a failed test measured with a lopsided metric.** The 50% bar passes in 1 of 3 agencies, on 3 to 11 programs, the tool is below a random draw from its own top 50 for DEQ and DHHS, and the "ever flagged in any window" score favours any method that rotates through many programs (F1, F2, F3). Tradeoff to name: a short shortlist that repeats the same items (84 distinct notices over two years for DEQ) is easy to read but misses most of what changes; a long rotating list catches more but is the manual monitoring the sponsor already has. Say which side the pilot is choosing.
2. **Fit is a vocabulary overlap normalised so every agency's best item reads 1.00, and it is only as good as each plan's text.** Absolute scores are 0.03 to 0.16, plans differ 16x in length, and weak-plan agencies get confident-looking nonsense (F4, F5, F14). Tradeoff: keyword TF-IDF is transparent and needs no outside service, but it cannot tell "no good match" from "a good match"; an absolute floor makes the tool say "nothing this period" more often, which is honest but looks less useful in a demo.
3. **The two cases that sell the pitch are not model results.** DERA is a hand lift to 0.8 (model rank 84 for DEQ), with no NC award history to back it, and the won-set that supports the backtest includes formula lines, one-off old awards and de-obligations (F6, F11). Tradeoff: a program map lets staff encode knowledge the text cannot, and costs the claim that the ranking is automatic and unbiased; say plainly that mapped pairs are human-placed.
